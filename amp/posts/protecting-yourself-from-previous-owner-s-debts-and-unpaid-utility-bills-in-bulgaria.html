Protecting Yourself from Previous Owner's Debts and Unpaid Utility Bills in Bulgaria

ZenithZero
11.09.2026

Purchasing a property in Bulgaria is an exciting venture, whether you are drawn to the coastal breeze of the Black Sea or the snowy peaks of the mountains. However, navigating the legalities of real estate acquisition can sometimes present unexpected hurdles. One of the most stressful surprises for foreign and domestic buyers alike is discovering that the previous owner left behind a mountain of unpaid utility bills, municipal taxes, or hidden debts. In worst-case scenarios, these liabilities can disrupt your peace of mind and lead to service cut-offs or legal disputes.

Understanding how Bulgarian law handles property-tied debts, performing rigorous due diligence, and taking preventative measures during the buying process are essential steps to safeguard your investment. This comprehensive guide will walk you through everything you need to know to protect yourself from inheriting someone else's financial baggage in Bulgaria.

The Legal Reality of Property Debts in Bulgaria

A common misconception among first-time property buyers is that utility debts and municipal obligations automatically attach to the person who incurred them rather than the physical property itself. In Bulgarian real estate practice, the situation is nuanced. While utility supply contracts are technically personal agreements between the utility company and the account holder (the previous owner), utility providers often make it difficult for new owners to open fresh accounts until past balances are cleared, or they threaten to cut off services.

Furthermore, local taxes and fees—such as the annual municipal garbage collection fee (такса битови отпадъци) and real estate tax—are directly linked to the property. If these remain unpaid, the municipality holds a legal claim over the real estate, meaning liens can potentially be enforced regardless of who currently holds the title deed.

Common Types of Unpaid Bills and Liabilities

Before finalizing any purchase agreement, you must investigate several categories of potential debt associated with the property:

  • Electricity Bills (EVN, CEZ/Electrohold, Energo-Pro): Unpaid power bills are the most frequent issue. While electricity companies cannot legally force you to pay the previous owner’s debt out of your own pocket, they often delay transferring the meter into your name until the account is settled, leaving you without power.
  • Water and Sewage (V&K): Similar to electricity, water supply companies are notorious for holding out on account transfers until historical arrears are paid.
  • Heating and Central Hot Water (Toplofikatsiya): Particularly relevant in larger cities like Sofia or for those Buying an Apartment in Bansko: Four-Season Living and Utility Bills, district heating debts can accumulate into massive sums due to winter usage and late penalties.
  • Municipal Taxes: Local taxes owed to the municipality where the property is located.
  • Homeowners Association (HOA) Fees: Fees owed to the building management or condominium association for maintenance, cleaning, and elevator repairs.

Step-by-Step Due Diligence to Prevent Inheritance of Debt

Mitigating the risk of inheriting utility arrears relies entirely on thorough pre-acquisition checks. Never take a seller's or a broker's verbal assurance that "all bills are paid" at face value.

1. Request Recent Utility Statements

Ask the seller to provide copies of the latest monthly bills for electricity, water, and heating, alongside official receipts proving payment. Verify that the customer numbers match the property you intend to buy.

2. Visit Utility Providers in Person

Accompanied by your lawyer or translator (or utilizing a power of attorney), visit the local customer service centers of EVN, Electrohold, Energo-Pro, or the local water utility (V&K). Request an official certificate or written statement confirming that there are no outstanding balances on the specific subscriber numbers.

3. Check Municipal Tax Clearance

By Bulgarian law, a notary will require a tax evaluation certificate (данъчна оценка) issued by the local municipality to complete the final purchase transaction. However, this document does not always automatically highlight historical arrears unless explicitly requested. Ensure your legal representative conducts a thorough search at the local municipality’s revenue department.

4. Consult the Condominium Management

For apartments within managed complexes or residential blocks, request a written certificate of good standing from the building manager (домоуправител) or the management company. This document should certify that all monthly maintenance fees have been paid in full up to the date of transfer.

Contractual Safeguards in the Preliminary Contract

The preliminary contract (предварителен договор) is your primary legal shield during a Bulgarian property transaction. Your lawyer should draft specific clauses to protect you from unexpected liabilities.

Contract Clause Purpose Legal Implication
Declaration of Zero Debt Seller legally declares that no utility or tax arrears exist. Provides grounds for breach of contract claims if false.
Retention of Funds Holding a portion of the purchase price in an escrow account until utility accounts are successfully transferred. Ensures leverage to pay off any discovered discrepancies using the seller's funds.
Indemnification Clause Explicitly states the seller is responsible for any pre-closing debts. Allows you to sue the seller for full financial recovery if third parties demand payment from you.

What to Do If You Discover Debts After the Purchase

Despite your best efforts, surprises can still happen. If you find yourself facing unpaid bills after signing the title deed at the notary, take immediate action:

  • Do Not Panic-Pay Immediately: Unless it is absolutely necessary to restore critical services and your lawyer advises it for expediency, avoid blindly paying debts that legally belong to someone else.
  • Leverage the Notary Deed and Contract: Present your title deed and the clauses of your preliminary contract to the utility provider to prove you are a new, distinct legal entity acquiring the property unencumbered.
  • Pursue Legal Recourse: If utility companies or municipal bodies aggressively pursue you for the previous owner's debts, engage a Bulgarian attorney to issue formal objections or file recourse claims against the seller based on contractual breach of warranties.

Conclusion

Protecting yourself from a previous owner's debts and unpaid utility bills in Bulgaria requires vigilance, legal foresight, and strict adherence to due diligence protocols. By thoroughly inspecting utility accounts, securing tax clearance certificates, embedding robust protective clauses into your preliminary contract, and working alongside trusted legal professionals, you can ensure that your Bulgarian property acquisition remains a rewarding and stress-free investment.
