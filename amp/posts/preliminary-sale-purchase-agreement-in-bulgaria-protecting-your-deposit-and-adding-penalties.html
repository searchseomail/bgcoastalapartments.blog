Preliminary Sale-Purchase Agreement in Bulgaria: Protecting Your Deposit and Adding Penalties

NeonGlitch
04.10.2026

Purchasing real estate in Bulgaria is an exciting venture, whether you are looking for a sunny coastal retreat, an investment property, or a permanent home. However, before any final title deeds are signed in front of a notary, the standard legal procedure involves signing a Preliminary Sale-Purchase Agreement (Predvaritelen dogovor). This document acts as a binding roadmap for the transaction, bridging the gap between the initial handshake and the official transfer of ownership. For foreign buyers and locals alike, understanding the nuances of this contract—specifically how to safeguard your deposit and enforce legal penalties—is critical to a successful and stress-free acquisition.

Navigating the Bulgarian property market requires careful due diligence, especially when substantial sums of money change hands early in the process. From verifying property ownership to structuring protective payment milestones, every clause matters. In this comprehensive guide, we will break down the mechanics of the preliminary agreement, explore strategies for securing your funds, and examine how penalty clauses can keep both buyers and sellers legally accountable.

What is a Preliminary Sale-Purchase Agreement in Bulgaria?

Under the Bulgarian Obligations and Contracts Act, a preliminary contract for the purchase of real estate contains all the essential elements of the final deal: the identification of the parties, the detailed description of the property, the agreed purchase price, and the timeline for completion. While it does not transfer formal ownership title—which can only be done via a Notarial Deed—it creates a mutual legal obligation for both buyer and seller to proceed with the final transaction.

Why is this document so important? Real estate transactions rarely happen overnight. Sellers often need time to gather documentation, clear minor encumbrances, or finalize construction (in the case of off-plan properties). Buyers usually need time to arrange financing, transfer funds internationally, or conduct thorough legal checks. The preliminary agreement locks in the price and terms, legally preventing either party from backing out arbitrarily or raising the price midway through the process.

Legal Weight and Court Enforcement

One of the most powerful aspects of a preliminary contract under Bulgarian law is Article 19, Paragraph 3 of the Obligations and Contracts Act. If one party unjustifiably refuses to sign the final notarial deed after the preliminary contract has been executed, the aggrieved party has the right to bring a lawsuit before a Bulgarian court to declare the preliminary contract final. Through this legal action, the court can essentially force the transfer of ownership, making the judgment act as the title deed itself.

Protecting Your Deposit (Kapara)

When signing the preliminary agreement, the buyer typically pays a deposit, commonly referred to as a "kapara" (earnest money), which generally amounts to 10% of the total purchase price. This money demonstrates serious intent, but it also represents a significant financial risk if things go wrong. Proper legal drafting is essential to ensure your hard-earned cash is fully protected.

Before handing over any funds, it is vital to perform comprehensive legal checks. You should check a property in Bulgaria for debts, liens, and encumbrances to ensure the seller has the undisputed right to sell and that no hidden mortgages, third-party claims, or state debts are tied to the asset.

Key Protections for Your Deposit

  • Escrow Accounts: Instead of transferring the deposit directly to the seller’s personal account—especially if dealing with private sellers or off-plan developers—consider utilizing a specialized escrow account managed by a lawyer or bank until specific conditions are met.
  • Refund Conditions: Explicitly outline scenarios under which the deposit must be returned in full. This includes negative findings during the title search, failure of the seller to provide necessary legal paperwork, or refusal of a bank mortgage application (if the purchase is mortgage-contingent).
  • Clear Receipt Documentation: Ensure that every cash or bank transfer is acknowledged in writing, either directly within the text of the preliminary agreement or via a signed, dated annex/bank transfer confirmation specifying that the amount serves as a deposit for the specific property.

The Role and Structure of Penalty Clauses

To ensure that both buyer and seller adhere strictly to the timelines and commitments outlined in the preliminary agreement, penalty clauses ( неустойka / neustoyka) are embedded into the contract. These clauses dictate the financial consequences of a breach of contract, such as delayed payments, late delivery, or a sudden withdrawal from the deal.

Under Bulgarian contract law, penalties must be reasonable and balanced. If a penalty is deemed excessively disproportionate to the damages incurred, a Bulgarian court may reduce it upon request. Therefore, drafting these clauses requires precision and a clear understanding of local legal standards.

Scenario Standard Legal Consequence Recommended Penalty Clause Structure
Buyer Withdraws Unjustifiably Buyer loses the deposit (kapara). If the buyer defaults, the seller keeps the deposit as liquidated damages.
Seller Withdraws Unjustifiably Seller must return the deposit. Seller must return the deposit double (or pay a matching penalty equivalent to 10-20% of the price).
Delayed Payment by Buyer Statutory interest on late payments. A daily penalty of 0.05% to 0.1% of the overdue installment amount.
Delayed Completion/Handover by Seller General claims for damages (hard to prove in court). A daily penalty for every day of delay past the agreed Act 15/Act 16 or notary date.

Double Deposit Return (Return of Kapara in Double Amount)

A classic feature of Bulgarian property law concerning the standard "kapara" is that if the party who received the deposit (the seller) defaults on the agreement, they are legally bound to return it to the buyer in a **double amount**. Ensuring this principle is explicitly written into your preliminary agreement gives you powerful financial leverage and discourages sellers from accepting higher offers from third-party buyers at the eleventh hour.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid in Bulgarian Preliminary Contracts

Drafting a preliminary agreement without professional legal representation can lead to costly mistakes. Here are some of the most frequent traps foreign and domestic buyers fall into:

  • Vague Property Descriptions: Make sure the property is identified not just by its address, but by its official identifier (cadastral number), exact square footage, adjoining storage rooms (cellars/attics), and ideal parts of the land.
  • Unrealistic Timelines: Build buffer periods into the payment schedule and the final notary date, especially if you are coordinating international bank transfers or awaiting bureaucratic certificates.
  • Ignoring Construction Milestones: If buying off-plan (properties under construction), tie your installment payments strictly to official construction stages defined under Bulgarian law, namely **Act 14** (rough construction/structural completion), **Act 15** (statement of completion), and **Act 16** (Permission to Use certificate).
