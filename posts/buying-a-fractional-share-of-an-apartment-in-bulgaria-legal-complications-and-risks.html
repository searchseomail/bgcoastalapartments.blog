Buying a Fractional Share of an Apartment in Bulgaria: Legal Complications and Risks

CyberNomad99
13.09.2026 • Read time: 5 min

The allure of owning a slice of the Black Sea coastline or a ski-resort studio in the Balkan nation has long captivated foreign investors. With relatively low entry barriers compared to Western European markets, property acquisition here seems straightforward. However, a growing trend among budget-conscious buyers is the purchase of fractional shares—buying a percentage of a single apartment rather than the whole title. While this fractional ownership model promises reduced costs and shared maintenance burdens, diving into this niche without understanding the local legal landscape can turn a dream holiday investment into a bureaucratic and financial nightmare. Navigating the nuances of Bulgarian property law requires extreme diligence, especially when multiple owners share a single title deed.

Understanding Fractional Ownership Under Bulgarian Property Law

In legal terms, fractional ownership in Bulgaria is governed by the concept of "Co-ownership" (Собственост върху идеални части). When you buy a fractional share, you do not own a specific room or a physical corner of the apartment; instead, you own an "ideal part" (idealna chast) of the entire property. This means you hold a percentage undivided share of the living room, kitchen, walls, and the underlying land (if applicable).

While the Bulgarian Property Act legally recognizes co-ownership, it does not inherently provide a smooth operational framework for vacation apartments shared by strangers or acquaintances. Every major decision regarding the property requires consensus or specific majorities, which can quickly lead to gridlock when co-owners disagree on usage, renovations, or sale terms.

Major Legal Complications and Risks

1. The Right of First Purchase (Preemption Rights)

One of the most significant hurdles in fractional ownership in Bulgaria is the statutory right of first refusal. Under Article 33 of the Property Act, if a co-owner decides to sell their ideal part to a third party, they must first offer it to the other existing co-owners under the exact same conditions.

  • The seller must present a notarized invitation detailing the offer and the price.
  • Other co-owners have a legal window of two months to respond and exercise their right.
  • If a co-owner sells their share to an outsider without properly offering it to the other co-owners first, the sale can be challenged in court, and the aggrieved co-owner can legally step into the shoes of the buyer.

2. Physical Usage and Scheduling Conflicts

Owning a 25% share of a coastal apartment does not automatically grant you the right to use it during the peak summer months of July and August. Unless there is a meticulously drafted and notarized internal usage agreement (agreement for distribution of use, known in Bulgarian as *razpredelenie na polzvaneto*), disputes over who gets to use the property during holidays are entirely inevitable.

Without a court-approved or mutually signed usage schedule, any co-owner can legally occupy the property at any time, potentially leading to overlapping demands and bitter standoffs.

3. Maintenance, Utility Bills, and Hidden Physical Defects

Co-owners are jointly and severally liable for taxes, municipal fees, and maintenance costs proportional to their shares. However, tracking down delinquent co-owners who refuse to pay their share of utility bills or building maintenance fees can be exhausting. Furthermore, buying fractional shares often happens hastily without proper technical inspections. Buyers should be acutely aware that shared properties—particularly near the sea—frequently suffer from structural vulnerabilities, meaning you could be forced to pay for expensive repairs while dealing with mold, humidity, and insulation issues in Bulgarian coastline apartments that accumulated due to prolonged periods of vacancy.

The Pitfalls of Remote Transactions and Power of Attorney

Many fractional share schemes are marketed online to international buyers. This often leads investors to complete transactions without ever stepping foot in the country. Relying on local intermediaries or developers to handle the paperwork via a proxy introduces severe vulnerabilities. Engaging in a remote property purchase in Bulgaria requires absolute trust in an independent local lawyer, as generic power of attorney documents can easily be exploited to the detriment of foreign buyers who do not read or speak Bulgarian.

Comparative Overview: Whole Ownership vs. Fractional Shares in Bulgaria

Feature Whole Property Ownership Fractional Share Ownership
Decision Making 100% autonomous control over use and sale. Requires consensus or strict adherence to co-ownership laws.
Exit Strategy Straightforward sale on the open market. Subject to co-owners' right of first refusal, limiting liquidity.
Legal Complexity Standard title verification and notary transfer. High risk of hidden disputes, encumbrances, and usage deadlocks.
Financing & Mortgages Easier to secure local bank financing. Virtually impossible to mortgage a fractional share.

How to Mitigate Risks Before Buying

If you still wish to proceed with purchasing a fractional share of a Bulgarian apartment, you must take proactive legal safeguards to protect your investment:

  • Hire an Independent Attorney: Never use the developer's or seller's recommended lawyer. Hire an independent Bulgarian property lawyer who specializes in co-ownership disputes.
  • vThoroughly Review the Property History: Check the Property Register (Имотен регистър) to ensure there are no hidden mortgages, executive liens, or pending lawsuits against other co-owners tied to the same title deed.
  • Draft a Binding Usage Agreement: Insist on creating a comprehensive internal usage contract that explicitly defines time-sharing periods, maintenance cost splits, and protocols for renting out the property to tourists.
  • Evaluate Market Realities: Remember that fractional shares are notoriously illiquid. When comparing market trends, keep in mind that unlike standard resale apartments vs off-plan developments in Bulgaria, fractional parts hold virtually no resale value and are exceptionally difficult to liquidate during economic downturns.

Conclusion

Buying a fractional share of an apartment in Bulgaria may seem like a clever hack for securing a budget-friendly European holiday home, but the legal complexities and operational risks far outweigh the initial savings. From statutory preemption rights and scheduling conflicts to joint liabilities and exit bottlenecks, co-ownership creates a minefield for the unprepared investor. Thorough legal counsel, airtight contracts, and a clear exit strategy are absolute prerequisites if you choose to venture down this challenging path.
