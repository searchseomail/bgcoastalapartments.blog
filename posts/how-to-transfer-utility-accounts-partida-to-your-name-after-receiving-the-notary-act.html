How to Transfer Utility Accounts (Partida) to Your Name After Receiving the Notary Act

EchoChamber
22.09.2026 • Read time: 8 min

Securing the Title Deed (Notary Act) for your newly purchased property marks a monumental milestone, whether you have invested in a coastal retreat, a mountain cabin, or urban real estate. However, holding the physical or digital paperwork is only half the battle. To truly make the property your own and ensure uninterrupted living conditions, you must complete the administrative process of transferring the utility accounts—known locally in countries like Bulgaria as the partida—into your name.

Failing to update these accounts can lead to severe logistical headaches, including delayed or missing bills, continued liability for the previous owner's consumption, and potential service disconnections. This comprehensive guide walks you through the exact steps, required documentation, timelines, and nuances of transferring utility meters (electricity, water, and heating) after your property purchase.

Why Transferring the Partida Immediately Matters

Many new property owners mistakenly assume that once the Notary Act is signed and registered with the local registry agency, utilities automatically switch over. Unfortunately, utility companies operate independently from the real estate registry. Until you formally notify them of the ownership change, the accounts remain tied to the seller.

Leaving utilities in the previous owner’s name creates several risks:

  • Unpaid Debts: If the previous owner leaves outstanding balances, utility companies may target the property itself with service suspensions, regardless of who currently holds the title.
  • Communication Breakdown: Bills and notices will continue to go to the old owner, leaving you blind to your consumption rates and payment deadlines.
  • Legal Complications: In certain jurisdictions, proving utility management is required for residency applications, local tax registrations, and calculating your realistic annual running costs of a one-bedroom apartment in Bulgaria.

Overview of the Required Documentation

While exact administrative requirements vary depending on the municipality and specific utility providers (electricity, water, gas, and district heating), a standard dossier of documents is universally required. Before visiting customer service centers, ensure you have gathered and, where necessary, translated or copied the following items:

  • The Original Notary Act (Notarial Deed): Proof of your legal ownership. You will need to show the original, and providers usually keep a copy.
  • Proof of Identity: A valid passport or national ID card for all registered owners.
  • Tax Evaluation Certificate (Udostoverenie za danets): Often requested to confirm the property's tax status with the local municipality.
  • Protocol of Handover (Protokol za predavane): A signed document between you and the seller detailing meter serial numbers and final readings on the day of the transfer.
  • Application Forms: Provided by the respective utility company on-site or downloaded from their official web portal.

Step-by-Step Guide to Transferring Utility Accounts

Step 1: Conduct the Meter Reading and Sign a Handover Protocol

The moment you receive your keys and finalize the purchase at the notary office (you can learn more about these initial legal steps through resources detailing notarial involvement when purchasing real estate), you should immediately visit the utility meters with the seller. Record the exact meter numbers and their current digital or analog readings. Both parties should sign a written handover protocol. This protects you from paying for electricity or water consumed prior to your official date of acquisition.

Step 2: Settle Final Bills with the Previous Owner

Ensure that the seller has paid all bills up to the date of the handover protocol. Ask for payment receipts. Utility companies will typically refuse to initiate a name transfer on an account that carries an active debt balance. Clear communication during this phase prevents unnecessary delays.

Step 3: Visit the Electricity Provider

Electricity is usually the first and most critical utility to transfer. In regions undergoing rapid development—such as properties near capital city peripheries, exemplified by developments discussed in insights on luxury properties and gated communities at the foot of Vitosha—managing electrical loads and provider administration efficiently is vital.

Bring your Notary Act, ID, and the handover protocol to the customer care center of your regional electricity distributor. Fill out the request for a customer profile change. You will typically pay a small administrative fee, and the system will register your name for future billing cycles.

Step 4: Update the Water and Sanitation Account (Voda)

Water utility companies require a similar procedure. You must present the Notary Act and the water meter reading. Note that some water authorities require a certificate proving that the property has no outstanding local municipal liabilities. Ensure your local municipal taxes are paid up to date to avoid being turned away.

Step 5: Address Auxiliary Services (Heating, Gas, and Internet)

If your property relies on centralized district heating (common in urban apartments) or bottled/piped gas, repeat the process with the local heating monopoly. Additionally, do not forget to contact telecommunication providers if you wish to retain, cancel, or transfer internet and television lines left by the previous occupant.

Timeline and Practical Summary

To give you a clear roadmap, the table below outlines the general timeline, responsible parties, and key actions required for a seamless utility transition.

Phase Action Item Recommended Timeline
Pre-Transfer Sign Handover Protocol & record meter numbers Day of Notary Act signing
Verification Confirm previous owner has zero balance Within 1 week of purchase
Execution Submit documents to Electricity & Water providers Within 2–4 weeks of receiving Notary Act
Finalization Verify first bill arrives in your name Next billing cycle (usually 30 days later)

Tips for Foreign Owners and Remote Managers

If you are a non-resident buyer—such as digital nomads establishing a base, or foreign investors purchasing holiday homes—navigating local utility offices can present a language barrier. Many buyers choose to grant a Power of Attorney (POA) to their local lawyer, real estate agent, or property manager to handle these bureaucratic tasks on their behalf.

Furthermore, if you plan to split your time between different regions, such as combining coastal living with remote work hubs—much like expats exploring buying sea-panorama villas in Sozopol or seasonal mountain towns—setting up online customer profiles with your utility providers is essential. Digital portals allow you to monitor consumption remotely, pay bills via credit card, and receive email notifications, ensuring your property remains fully managed even when you are miles away.

By following these structured steps diligently right after your notary appointment, you will secure your property rights fully, protect yourself from unexpected financial liabilities, and enjoy a hassle-free transition into your new home.
