Understanding the Notary Act in Bulgaria: Key Clauses You Should Never Overlook

ZenithZero
06.08.2026 • Read time: 7 min

Navigating the Bulgarian real estate market can be an exciting venture, especially given the country's alluring coastal landscapes, affordable property prices, and welcoming environment for international investors. However, the culmination of any property purchase in Bulgaria is not the handshake or even the preliminary contract—it is the signing of the Notary Act (Notarialen Akt). This official document is the ultimate proof of ownership, recorded in the Bulgarian Property Register. Yet, many buyers dive into the process without fully grasping the complex legal terminology and critical clauses contained within this binding document.

Whether you are purchasing a cozy holiday apartment by the Black Sea or a sprawling rural estate, understanding the fine print of the Bulgarian Notary Act is non-negotiable. Overlooking critical clauses regarding property encumbrances, payment schedules, or structural boundaries can lead to costly legal disputes down the line. To ensure a seamless acquisition, especially if you are navigating the market from abroad, it helps to follow a Step-by-Step Guide to Buying Property in Bulgaria as a Foreigner, while keeping a close eye on the specific legal mechanisms detailed below.

What is a Bulgarian Notary Act?

The Notary Act is a public document drawn up by a licensed Bulgarian notary public to formalize the transfer of real estate ownership from a seller to a buyer. In Bulgaria, property ownership rights do not officially transfer upon signing a private preliminary contract; the transfer only becomes legally binding and recognized by the state the exact moment the Notary Act is signed by all parties, verified by the notary, and entered into the Property Register maintained by the Registry Agency.

Because the notary acts as an impartial state official verifying legality rather than protecting the individual interests of either the buyer or the seller, having your own legal representation is vital. Many buyers wonder about professional assistance, and asking Do You Need an Independent Lawyer to Buy an Apartment in Bulgaria or Is a Broker Enough? is one of the first questions you should answer to protect your capital before stepping into the notary’s office.

Anatomy of the Notary Act: Key Sections and Clauses

A standard Bulgarian Notary Act follows a rigorous template established by Bulgarian property law. While it may look uniform at first glance, the specific clauses inserted into the document dictate your rights and liabilities as a property owner. Here are the core sections you must review meticulously:

1. Identification of Parties and Representation

This section outlines the personal details of the buyer(s) and seller(s), including full names, birth dates, permanent addresses, and EGN (Personal Number for Foreigners) or LNCh numbers. If either party is represented by a third party via Power of Attorney (POA), the act must explicitly reference the POA document, its date, and the notary who certified it. Ensure that the POA grants specific rights to buy or sell the exact property in question; general powers of attorney are often rejected or scrutinized.

2. Detailed Property Description (Technical Characteristics)

The Notary Act must contain an unambiguous description of the real estate. This includes:

  • The exact geographical location (municipality, city/village, street address).
  • The type of property (apartment, house, land plot, commercial space).
  • Identifiers such as the property number (identifikator), building number, and floor.
  • Total built-up area (RZE - unfolded built-up area) and the functional breakdown of the rooms.
  • Adjacent spaces, such as basements (izbe), attics (tavani), and ideal parts of the land and common areas of the building.

3. Financial and Tax Valuation Clauses

Bulgarian law requires two separate valuations to be mentioned in the Notary Act: the notarial/market purchase price agreed upon by the parties, and the tax evaluation (danatsenna otsenka) issued by the local municipality. The declared purchase price cannot legally be lower than the municipal tax valuation. Stamp duty, notary fees, and local taxes are calculated based on the higher of these two figures.

Crucial Clauses You Should Never Overlook

Beyond the standard administrative data, certain clauses carry immense legal and financial weight. Failing to review or negotiate these terms can result in hidden liabilities.

The "Free of Encumbrances" Clause (Chlen 33 and Title Guarantees)

The seller must explicitly declare and guarantee that the property is free from all mortgages, liens, injunctions, tax debts, third-party rights, or pending lawsuits. Before the signing, the notary conducts a background check using the Property Register to issue a "Certificate of Encumbrances" (Udostoverenie za tezhesti). However, you should independently verify that this clause explicitly protects you against any pre-existing claims from creditors of the seller.

Payment Confirmation and Method Clauses

How and when money changes hands must be documented with absolute precision. The Notary Act will typically state whether the purchase price has been paid in full prior to the signing, or if it is being paid on the day of signing. To comply with Bulgarian anti-money laundering regulations and ensure security, large transactions are almost exclusively executed via bank transfers. Managing this efficiently requires preparation, such as knowing How to Open a Bulgarian Bank Account for Property Transactions as a Non-Resident well in advance of the closing date.

Warranties for Hidden Defects and Eviction

Under Bulgarian law, sellers are implicitly responsible for hidden defects that diminish the value or normal use of the property. However, standard contracts sometimes attempt to limit this liability. Ensure that the Notary Act does not contain waivers that strip away your statutory rights to compensation if structural flaws, unpermitted modifications, or boundary overlaps are discovered post-purchase.

Summary of Costs and Financial Obligations Associated with the Notary Act

Signing the Notary Act triggers immediate statutory fees and taxes. It is standard practice in Bulgaria for the buyer to cover these costs, though parties can negotiate otherwise. Below is a breakdown of the typical financial obligations:

Common Pitfalls to Avoid Before Signing

To safeguard your investment, keep these final strategic points in mind:

  • Skipping the Draft Review: Never read the Notary Act for the first time at the notary’s desk. Insist that your lawyer reviews a draft at least 48 hours prior to the appointment.
  • Ignoring Ideal Parts and Common Areas: Ensure that the rights to use surrounding land, parking spots, or shared corridors are explicitly written into the act, especially when buying into gated communities or apartment complexes.
  • Language Barriers: If you are not fluent in Bulgarian, the law mandates that a sworn, licensed translator must be present during the signing to read and translate the document aloud before you sign.

By approaching the Notary Act with meticulous attention to detail, rigorous legal support, and a clear understanding of its core clauses, you can successfully secure your property in Bulgaria with total peace of mind.
