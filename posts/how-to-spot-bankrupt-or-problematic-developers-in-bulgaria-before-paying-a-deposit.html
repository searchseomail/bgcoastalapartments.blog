Bulgarian Real Estate

How to Spot Bankrupt or Problematic Developers in Bulgaria Before Paying a Deposit

RetroRider
10.09.2026 • Read time: 4 min

Buying off-plan or newly built real estate in Bulgaria can be an exciting venture, offering stunning Black Sea views, peaceful mountain retreats, or lucrative rental investments. However, the Bulgarian property market—particularly along the coast and around major cities—has historically experienced cycles of rapid development, followed by stalled projects, developer insolvencies, and legal disputes. Handing over a holding deposit without performing rigorous due diligence is one of the riskiest mistakes a buyer can make. Before you commit your hard-earned capital, you must learn how to independently investigate the financial health, legal standing, and reputation of the construction company or developer.

1. The Bulgarian Commercial Register: Your First Line of Defense

Every legitimate company operating in Bulgaria must be registered with the Bulgarian Commercial Register (Registry Agency). This public database is a goldmine of information regarding a developer's financial stability and corporate history. Checking the company profile should always be your starting point.

Key Financial Documents to Analyze

Bulgarian companies are legally required to submit their annual financial statements (annual reports, balance sheets, and profit-and-loss statements) to the register. When evaluating a developer:

  • Look for chronic losses: A company reporting consecutive years of net losses may be struggling with cash flow.
  • Examine liabilities vs. assets: If the developer has massive short-term debts compared to liquid assets or ongoing projects, they may be relying entirely on your deposit to fund previous debts (a classic Ponzi-like construction trap).
  • Check for capital decreases: Sudden drops in registered capital can indicate asset stripping or financial distress.

Corporate Red Flags in the Registry

Pay close attention to the corporate history and structural changes within the company. Frequent changes of managers, shifting registered addresses to virtual offices, or recent transfers of company ownership to individuals with no prior real estate experience often signal that the original owners are attempting to evade liabilities or push a failing company into artificial bankruptcy.

2. Checking for Pending Lawsuits and Executive Proceedings

A developer might look prosperous on paper while drowning in court battles. You need to verify whether the company, its owners, or its associated entities are involved in civil, commercial, or enforcement cases.

You can check Bulgarian court portals (such as the Unified Portal of Bulgarian Courts) to see if the developer is a defendant in lawsuits brought by:

  • Unpaid contractors and subcontractors: If a developer cannot pay builders, construction will halt.
  • Disgruntled previous buyers: Lawsuits for failure to deliver properties on time or refusal to refund deposits are massive warning signs.
  • Banks and financial institutions: Foreclosure proceedings or executive cases initiated by bailiffs mean the developer’s assets—potentially including the land your future home sits on—may be seized.

3. Land Ownership and Encumbrances (The Notary Act and Property Deeds)

Before paying any deposit, you must verify who actually owns the land on which the building is planned or constructed. Never rely solely on the developer's word or marketing brochures.

Hire an independent local lawyer to pull a property certificate (Certificate of Encumbrances / Удостоверение за тежести) from the Registry Agency. This document will reveal:

  • Whether the land is mortgaged to a bank (if the developer took a construction loan using your future plot as collateral).
  • Whether there are active attachment orders (vazlahvane) placed by bailiffs due to the developer's debts.
  • Whether third parties hold rights over the property.

If the land is heavily mortgaged or encumbered, your deposit could easily vanish if the bank forecloses before the building receives its operational permit (Act 16).

4. Analyzing Past Project Delivery and Reputation

A track record of delayed projects is often the precursor to bankruptcy. Investigate what the developer has built previously:

  • Did they deliver past residential or holiday complexes on time, or are there projects lingering in limbo for years?
  • Did previous buyers receive their title deeds (Notary Acts) promptly after completion, or are there bureaucratic blockages?
  • What are the ongoing management realities in their finished complexes? For instance, hidden or exorbitant maintenance fees in Bulgarian seaside resorts are often a symptom of desperate developers or mismanaged property management companies trying to recover lost profits.

Visit completed projects in person if possible. Talk to current residents. They will quickly tell you about structural defects, poor insulation, water leaks, or disputes with the developer over communal areas.

5. Structuring the Deposit Safely

If you have completed your due diligence and decided to move forward, how you handle the deposit is critical to minimizing your risk. Never wire large sums directly to a personal account or an offshore shell company.

Ensure that:

  • The deposit is paid into the official corporate bank account of the exact legal entity named in the preliminary contract. (If you need guidance on setting up your own financial structures for the purchase, read our guide on how to open a Bulgarian bank account for property transactions as a non-resident).
  • The Preliminary Contract (Предварителен договор) contains a clear, enforceable clause detailing full refund conditions if the developer fails to secure vital building milestones (such as Act 14 or Act 15) by specific dates.
  • An independent, licensed Bulgarian attorney reviews every single word of the contract before you sign or pay a single lev.

Summary Checklist for Spotting Problematic Developers

Indicator Safe Sign Danger Sign
Financial Health Stable profits, clear balance sheets, adequate registered capital. Chronic losses, high short-term liabilities, sudden capital drops.
Legal Status Clean record, no active lawsuits or bailiff claims. Multiple lawsuits from contractors, buyers, or banks.
Land Status Owned outright by the developer or backed by transparent bank guarantees. Heavy mortgages, active attachments, or land owned by third parties with legal disputes.
Track Record Proven history of delivering projects on time with Act 16 issued. Stalled construction sites, delayed permits, angry online communities.

Whether you are buying a holiday flat on the coast or exploring houses for sale in Burgas suburbs, the rule remains the same: diligence beats excitement every time. Taking a few weeks to thoroughly vet the developer through official state registers and legal channels can save you thousands of euros and years of litigation stress.
